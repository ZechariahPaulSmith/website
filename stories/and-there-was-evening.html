AND THERE WAS EVENING...

----------------------------------------------------------------------
“Dear God,” my body shakes with uneasy breath—It's strange, returning here: the body of an old man; curled up and behaving like he were still a child; a child whose parents still dragged him to the local church every Sunday morning, pressed into uncomfortable, stiff clothing. They were doing what was best—what they thought was best—for them; for him —that little boy—for me; and always for that miraculous creature in the sky. I can't blame them, having been sold the same scheme when they were little. I only chose later to buy into other lies that I too would pass on thinking I was doing right. Luckily I have no small brains to influence. For many, such brains would be a physical manifestation of hope—or fear depending how you look at it. Hope that in some way you can live beyond your time, beyond the nothingness you fear; a piece of you still breathing while you, the real you, rests in a jar long forgotten in an attic: cold and unfeeling ashes; a quickly forgotten monument to the long forgotten— or perhaps never known—meaning that you managed, or didn't manage, to dredge out of your short existence, as short at stillbirth as it is at one hundred—in the end anyway.

I'm not sure why I'm reaching out after so much time; to this God I no longer believe in—if I ever truly did in the first place, which is doubtful. Less ever a believer and more an employee clocking in and out of his nightly job of asking the void for better things, sleepily and unwillingly partaking in weekly Sunday meetings where plans are discussed by all present and understood by few, though all will proceed as if they know without a doubt the right thing to do next. I could have never belonged to that life: life without doubts; or I doubt I could have ever, and certainly not now. Just more doubt I guess—an endless disease. But even now I still ask, despite that doubt, not for anything yet, other than His attention, but perhaps concurrently requesting—begging for—His existence; for the existence of someone who could help me out of this place. Why I assume He could I don't know. I guess because I've 'known' him—as much as a doubter can—and find comfort in this return to a simpler life; one where my parent's could be a flight of steps away discussing the weather, dinner, or whatever was happening with the neighbors across the way, and I know they're gone. I know I live somewhere else now, but sometimes the lie is nice, if poisoned by the inevitable pain of its disappearance and the knowledge that nothing has ever come of my requests to Him; a lone voice in the dark and quiet night only answered by the limited echo of the small room. Why then do I ask Him? Why not ask for another God? For any God?

“Dear Gods,” I could start. I'm not sure which I refer to, but any that can hear me are good enough. How to ask I'm not sure, but I hope they'll forgive my lack of training in belief of them—but I can always learn should they choose to tell me which they are, though I'm suppose I'm also unsure how Gods other than the one I'm used to might go about showing themselves-. With 'my' God it always seemed that people saw Him where they wanted to: in the breeze, the rolling sea, or the brick exposed behind scraped off paint; always happening to tell them what they wanted to hear; that their current course of action—what they wanted—was not only justified, but ordained. I don't know what I want, Gods, and I don't know how to get it—and maybe that's why you've always been so quiet—but to be happy: to see the sun rise on a new day and be left in awe over dread, to see the beauty and not the sadness that events to come always bring, the hurt and the sorrow, death and politics, the endless cycle of pain I had no choice in joining; to know of these things and still hope for a future could be nice. But I need help to get there—why else would I be here, talking to you, or you all, or whoever... because that's just it, isn't it? I can admit it. I need help. I can't do this on my own and I'll take anyone—you don't have to be a God!

“Dear anyone who can hear me,” this is ridiculous, I believe in mind-readers as much as I believe in Gods, but if I stoop to asking one, I might as well ask the other. Hello? Can anybody hear me? Can someone knock on my door? Throw rocks at my window? Let me know that you can hear me? That someone—anyone—is there?

Of course not. My onslaught of deep sighs and quivering vocal chords unable to match the infinite champion silence.

I'm just being foolish—there are no Gods, no mind- readers, and the only one left is me. I am all that can hear the screams; the questions—all that can hear the cries.

I suppose I did ask for anyone—of which I shamefully apply. Perhaps it's a cruel joke, consciousness; a gift of sorts to answer the incurable need for companionship. A prayer necessarily answered before it's asked. A puzzle created and solved in nonsense, a nonsense founded by either man or God; it doesn't matter which for inevitably one invented the other.

Here I am: the only one asking to the only one listening for while Gods and mind-readers are under question, I must believe in man as these thoughts are mine and must mean something in process if not in content. And I have found that no one is coming to save me, least of all a stranger who listens, heavenly or not. If I want help, I'll have to help myself.

“Dear me,” lean up, shift the sheets—if only far enough to draw the curtain back; allow in dawn's dim light– already leaking through the gaps formed by the wave of the curtain's threads against the window's unfinished frame. You may not see its beauty yet, but to miss the sunrise altogether would prevent you from ever seeing if one day you might.

And there was morning.
